Project 1

Images of the
Russian Empire

Colorizing the Prokudin-Gorskii glass plate collection by aligning the three channels.

00

The problem

Around 1907, Sergei Prokudin-Gorskii photographed the Russian Empire in color by taking three exposures of every scene through a blue, a green and a red filter. The Library of Congress scanned the glass plates, and each scan is one tall grayscale image with the three exposures stacked. The job is to cut the scan into thirds, figure out how the green and red plates are shifted relative to the blue one, and stack them into a color image.

Everything below assumes a pure translation is enough: I only search over an integer (x, y) shift for each of the green and red plates and keep blue as the reference.

01

Single-scale alignment

The naive version is an exhaustive search. For every shift (dx, dy) in a window of [-15, 15] pixels in both directions, I roll the plate with np.roll, compare it to the blue plate with a similarity score, and keep the best shift. That is 31 x 31 = 961 comparisons per plate, which is nothing for the small JPEGs.

I implemented two scores:

L2 (Euclidean distance). sqrt(sum((a - b)^2)) over all pixels. Smaller is better. Cheap, but it directly compares brightness values, so it is thrown off when the two channels are simply exposed differently.

NCC (normalized cross-correlation). Subtract the mean from each image, divide by its norm, and take the dot product. Larger is better. Because of the normalization it does not care about a constant offset or scale in brightness, only about whether the two images go up and down in the same places.

Two details that matter: I crop 10% off every edge before scoring so the plate borders and the junk that np.roll wraps around from the other side do not dominate the score, and I convert everything to float in [0, 1] first.

All three low-resolution images align cleanly, and L2 and NCC agree on every offset here. Offsets are given as (x, y) in pixels, the amount the plate was moved to line up with blue.

02

Image pyramid

The full-resolution TIFF scans are about 3700 x 3200 pixels per plate, and the true offsets are up to ~180 pixels. A [-15, 15] window would miss them entirely, and widening it to [-200, 200] means 160,000 rolls of a 12-megapixel image. Too slow.

The fix is coarse-to-fine search. I build the pyramid myself: downsample both plates by 2 (area averaging, so it is anti-aliased), and recurse until the shorter side is under 400 pixels. At that coarsest level I run the same [-15, 15] exhaustive search. Walking back up, each level doubles the estimate from below and only searches a [-2, 2] window around it to fix the rounding. A 3700-pixel plate goes through three halvings, so the total work is one small brute-force search plus three 5 x 5 refinements on progressively larger images. Each TIFF takes about 10 seconds.

The recursion is a couple of lines: make_shift calls itself on the half-size plates, multiplies the answer by 2, and hands that to the single-scale search as the center of a tiny window. The single-scale function from part 01 is reused unchanged apart from the window and center arguments.

03

Where it fails: the Emir

The Emir's robe is a saturated blue. That makes it very bright in the blue plate and very dark in the red plate, so over the largest object in the frame the two channels are anti-correlated. Both L2 and NCC assume that matching content means matching brightness, and here that assumption is simply false. At the coarsest pyramid level NCC locks onto a wrong optimum and the small refinement windows on the way up cannot recover. L2 gets closer because the dark background wall still dominates its sum, but it does not land exactly either. The fix is to stop comparing brightness altogether, which is the first of the bells and whistles.

04

My own images from the collection

Three more plates downloaded from the Library of Congress collection (the ~1000 px JPEG versions, so they go through the single-scale search directly). All three align with both metrics.

05

Bells & whistles

Five automatic improvements on top of the basic pipeline. The first one changes what the alignment looks at; the other four are post-processing on the finished color image, applied in the order crop, color mapping, white balance, contrast.

05.1

Better features: aligning on edges

Instead of feeding raw intensities to the score, I feed the gradient magnitude of each plate: Sobel derivatives in x and y, combined as sqrt(gx^2 + gy^2). The robe is bright in one channel and dark in another, but its outline is in the same place in all three, so the edge maps correlate even where the brightness does not. The pyramid and the scoring code are untouched; only the input to make_shift changes, and the shift it returns is applied to the original plate.

With gradient features and NCC, all 17 images align. Every offset that was already correct stays within two pixels of the raw-pixel result, and emir goes from (-205, 141) to (40, 107).

05.2

Automatic cropping

The border has a property the picture does not: the three channels disagree there. In the real scene, red, green and blue rise and fall together; in a fringe, one channel is saturated and the others are not. So for each row and column I compute the mean channel spread, max(R,G,B) - min(R,G,B), and the mean brightness. Starting at each edge I walk inward until I find 20 consecutive rows (or columns) whose spread is below 1.3x the interior median and whose brightness is neither near black nor near white relative to the interior. That is the edge; I add a 1% safety margin and never remove more than 15% per side. Nothing is hard-coded per image.

05.3

Better color mapping

There is no reason the three filters Prokudin-Gorskii used match the primaries of sRGB. Real filters overlap: the "red" plate also records some green light and so on, so every recorded channel is a mix of the true ones and the result looks slightly washed out. Undoing a mix is a linear operation, so I apply a 3x3 matrix to every pixel. The matrix has a positive diagonal and small negative off-diagonals, which pulls each channel away from its neighbors, and every row sums to 1 so overall brightness is unchanged. I chose the coefficients by hand (1.15 on the diagonal, -0.05 to -0.10 off it); without a reference target there is nothing to fit against. The effect is a mild but visible increase in saturation, especially in the yellows and reds.

05.4

Automatic white balance

White balance has two halves: guess the color of the light, then divide it out. The second half is easy, a per-channel gain. For the first I use the gray-world assumption: averaged over the whole scene, the world is gray, so if the mean of the image is not gray, the difference is the illuminant. I compute the mean of each channel and scale each channel so its mean matches the average of the three. On most of these images that removes the warm or cyan cast the plates tend to have.

The assumption breaks when one color dominates the scene. siren is mostly purple flowers, so gray-world "corrects" them toward gray and the sky goes yellow. That is the known weakness of the method: it has no way to tell a colorful scene from a colored light.

05.5

Automatic contrast

The scans rarely use the full intensity range. I find the 1st and 99th percentile of all pixel values across the three channels and linearly map them to 0 and 1, clipping what falls outside. Percentiles instead of the true min and max so that a single dust speck or scratch does not set the range. It is one global stretch, not per channel, so the white balance from the previous step is preserved.

06

Final results

Gradient-feature NCC alignment followed by the full post-processing chain, same parameters for every image.
